Module One

Before the Beginning

Australia, Islam, and the small world of Australian jihadism before 2001.

1860s–2002 15 min Start here

On a winter Monday in June 2000, a 47-year-old Englishman living in Perth stood on a quiet street in Canberra and pointed a borrowed video camera at the Israeli embassy. He filmed the gates, the walls, the approaches. He was nervous and, by his own later account, not very good at it. Then he flew home, wrote up his notes, and waited for instructions that never came.

His name was Jack Roche. He had been born Paul George Holland in Hull, in the north of England, and he had converted to Islam in his forties after a long, unsettled life. A few months before he stood outside that embassy, he had been in Afghanistan, where he was taught how to make explosives and where he shook hands with Osama bin Laden.

Roche's plot went nowhere. He got cold feet. He tried, repeatedly, to telephone Australia's domestic intelligence agency to confess; nobody called him back. He was not arrested until November 2002 — after the Bali bombings, after everything changed — and when he was finally convicted in 2004 he became the first person in Australian history found guilty of a terrorism conspiracy on Australian soil.

It is a strange, almost comic beginning to a serious story. But it is the right place to start, because almost every feature of the twenty-five years that followed is already visible in it: a convert on the edges of a community; a foreign organisation that saw Australia as a soft target and a recruiting ground; a plot that was half-baked and yet perfectly real; and a state that did not yet know what it was looking at.

First, some ground clearing

Before going any further it is worth stating plainly what this account is and is not about, because the subject attracts a great deal of loose talk.

At the 2021 census, 813,392 people in Australia identified as Muslim — 3.2 per cent of the population. In 2001, at the start of this story, the figure was closer to 280,000, or one and a half per cent. They are not a bloc. They are Lebanese and Turkish and Afghan and Bangladeshi and Somali and Indonesian and Iraqi and Bosnian and Indian and Australian-born, Sunni and Shia and neither, secular and devout, and they disagree with one another about almost everything.

The number of Australians who have been convicted of jihadist terrorism offences over the whole period covered by this guide is in the low hundreds. The number who have actually killed someone in Australia in the name of the Islamic State, al-Qaeda or anything like them is smaller still — you could list them on a single page. Set against a population of 27 million, this is a very small phenomenon, and any honest account has to hold two things at once: that the numbers are tiny, and that the harm has nonetheless been real, sustained and, in December 2025, catastrophic.

A note on words

Jihadism is shorthand for a modern revolutionary ideology — sometimes called Salafi-jihadism — that holds violence to be an individual religious obligation for Muslims against both non-Muslim states and Muslim governments it regards as apostate. It is a twentieth-century political movement that uses religious language. It is held by a very small minority of Muslims and is rejected by the overwhelming majority of Islamic scholars. Module 10 gives a short primer; a fuller treatment belongs in its own piece.

Muslims in Australia: a very short history

Muslims have been in Australia longer than the Commonwealth has existed. Makassan fishermen from what is now Indonesia were visiting the north coast and trading with Yolŋu people for at least a century before European settlement. From the 1860s, cameleers from what is now Pakistan and Afghanistan — the men Australians lazily called "Afghans" — opened up the inland, built the first mosques in places like Marree and Broken Hill, and left their name on the Ghan railway. The White Australia policy then squeezed the community down to almost nothing.

The modern community is a product of post-war migration. Albanians and Bosnians came after the Second World War. A 1967 agreement brought Turkish workers, who settled heavily in Melbourne's northern suburbs and around Auburn in Sydney. The Lebanese civil war from 1975 brought a much larger wave, this time predominantly Sunni Muslim and predominantly poor, into a handful of suburbs in Sydney's south-west — Lakemba, Punchbowl, Bankstown, Wiley Park — and into Melbourne's north and west. Afghans came after the Soviet invasion and again after 2001; Somalis came in the 1990s; Iraqis came after 1991 and after 2003.

Two features of that history matter for what follows. The first is geographic concentration: a large share of Australia's Muslims live in a small number of local government areas in two cities, which means that social networks are dense and that a very small number of institutions — particular mosques, particular prayer rooms, particular bookshops — can carry disproportionate weight.

The second is economic disadvantage. The Lebanese wave in particular arrived as Australian manufacturing was collapsing. Unemployment in some of these suburbs ran at two and three times the national rate through the 1980s and 1990s. This does not explain terrorism — the great majority of people in those suburbs never went near it, and some of the most committed jihadists Australia has produced were middle-class converts — but it does explain the emergence of a young, alienated, heavily policed male subculture in south-west Sydney that later provided a recruiting pool.

The first fringe

Through the 1980s and 1990s a small, mostly harmless fringe developed at the edges of this community, and it came from two directions.

The first was Salafism: a purist, literalist reform movement, exported energetically from Saudi Arabia with Saudi money, which taught that Islam had been corrupted by centuries of accretion and should be returned to the practice of the first three generations of Muslims. Most Salafis are quietist and apolitical; a minority are not. Australian students went to Medina and Riyadh on scholarships and came back with new certainties and, often, considerable contempt for the sleepy Lebanese and Turkish mosques of their parents.

The second was the Afghan jihad. Through the 1980s, the war against the Soviet occupation of Afghanistan was a cause with broad international sympathy, backed by the United States and Saudi Arabia and Pakistan. A handful of Australians went. A handful more went to Bosnia in the early 1990s, and to Kashmir. The numbers were trivial — probably a few dozen people over fifteen years — but they created something that had not existed before: Australians with combat experience, foreign contacts and standing in a transnational movement.

By the late 1990s a recognisable scene existed. It had preachers, it had bookshops selling translated Arabic tracts, it had study circles in garages and community halls, and it had an emerging language of grievance built around Bosnia, Chechnya, Palestine and, above all, Iraq under sanctions. It was loud, it was small, and it was almost entirely legal — which was, as we will see, precisely the problem the Australian state would spend the next twenty-five years failing to solve.

Jemaah Islamiyah reaches Australia

The organisation that turned this scene into an operational problem was Indonesian.

Jemaah Islamiyah — "the Islamic Community" — grew out of the Darul Islam movement, a long-running Indonesian struggle for an Islamic state. Its founders, Abdullah Sungkar and Abu Bakar Bashir, fled Suharto's Indonesia in 1985 and spent more than a decade in exile in Malaysia. From there they built a regional organisation, sent men to train in Afghanistan, and divided their world into four mantiqi, or administrative regions.

Mantiqi 4 was Australia and Papua. It was run by Indonesian twin brothers, Abdul Rahim Ayub and Abdul Rahman Ayub, who arrived in the 1990s and set up in Perth and Sydney respectively. Its purpose was mostly logistical: raise money, gather materials, recruit. Sungkar and Bashir themselves visited Australia several times in the 1990s, speaking at mosques and private gatherings and collecting donations. At the time this attracted essentially no attention.

Being careful here

Most people who attended a talk by Abu Bakar Bashir in an Australian mosque in 1996 had no idea who he was and no interest in violence. Attendance at a gathering is not membership, and membership of a group that was not proscribed at the time was not an offence. This distinction — between a milieu and a conspiracy — runs through the whole of this story and is the single thing most often lost in public argument about it.

Roche's biography is a study in drift. Born into a poor family in Hull, in and out of children's homes, he came to Australia in the 1970s, worked in pubs and on building sites, drank heavily and married and divorced. In 1993, in Sydney, he converted to Islam. Within two years he had found his way into the orbit of the Ayub brothers, and in 1996 he swore an oath of allegiance to Abdullah Sungkar.

In March 2000 he was sent to Pakistan and then to Afghanistan. He spent time at a training camp near Kabul, learned to handle explosives, and was introduced to bin Laden and to al-Qaeda's military chief, Mohammed Atef. He was given money and a task: build a cell in Australia and hit Israeli targets.

He came home and did the surveillance. He filmed the Canberra embassy on 12 June 2000, using a video camera he had borrowed from a Sydney preacher named Feiz Mohammad — a man who reappears in Module 3, and who was never charged with any offence in relation to it. He drove past the Israeli consulate in Sydney. He wrote it all up and sent it on.

And then the thing fell apart. Roche later said that Bashir called the operation off. Whether or not that is right, Roche himself lost his nerve, went back to Perth, and started trying to tell someone. He rang the Australian Security Intelligence Organisation. He rang more than once. Nobody rang back. He gave up and went back to his life.

The most important fact about the Roche case is not that a plot existed. It is that when a man tried to hand it to the state, the state was not listening — because it did not yet believe there was anything to hear.

Roche was arrested in November 2002 and pleaded guilty in 2004. His nine-year sentence was widely criticised as light, and it probably was; the sentencing judge took into account the fact that he had tried to come forward. He was released in 2007, gave some interviews, and has lived quietly since.

What the law could and could not do

Here is the fact that most surprises people encountering this history for the first time: until 2002, Australia had no terrorism offences at all.

There was no crime of being a member of a terrorist organisation, no crime of preparing for a terrorist act, no mechanism for proscribing a group. Bombers could be charged with murder, or with explosives offences, or with conspiracy — but a person who trained abroad, came home, gathered materials and made plans committed no Commonwealth offence until he did something that was already a crime for other reasons. There was a Crimes (Foreign Incursions and Recruitment) Act from 1978, aimed at mercenaries and Croatian nationalists, which turned out to be more useful later than anyone expected. Otherwise, nothing.

This was not naivety so much as a particular reading of history. Australia had experienced political violence — the 1978 Hilton Hotel bombing in Sydney, the 1982 bombings of the Israeli consulate and the Hakoah Club in Sydney, the 1986 bombing of the Turkish consulate in Melbourne — but it had always been treated as ordinary crime, investigated by police, prosecuted under ordinary law. The prevailing assumption was that terrorism was something that happened elsewhere and occasionally splashed onto Australian soil.

Two dates

11 September 2001
New York and Washington

Ten Australians are among the 2,977 people killed. Prime Minister John Howard, who is in Washington that day, invokes the ANZUS treaty for the first time in its history. Australia commits forces to Afghanistan within weeks.

12 October 2002
Kuta, Bali

Jemaah Islamiyah bombs Paddy's Bar and the Sari Club. 202 people are killed, including 88 Australians. It remains the largest loss of Australian life to terrorism in history, and by a wide margin the event that shaped Australian counter-terrorism.

Bali did to Australia roughly what September 11 did to the United States, and it did it to a country that had spent the previous year telling itself that the threat was American. The dead were footballers and surf club members and school-leavers on the traditional end-of-year trip. The bombers were Indonesians from an organisation that had, it now emerged, been running a branch office in Sydney and Perth for the better part of a decade.

The legislative response was immediate and enormous. The Security Legislation Amendment (Terrorism) Act 2002 created, for the first time, a definition of a "terrorist act" and a family of offences built around it: committing one, planning one, financing one, training for one, belonging to an organisation that does them. Jemaah Islamiyah was formally listed as a terrorist organisation. In 2003, ASIO was given the power to detain and question people who were not themselves suspects. In 2005, after the London bombings, came control orders, preventative detention, and a further widening of the offences.

Between September 2001 and September 2021 the Australian Parliament passed 92 separate counter-terrorism statutes — a rate of law-making without parallel in comparable democracies. Whether this made Australia safer, and at what cost to ordinary liberties, is one of the genuinely open arguments in this story, and we return to it in Module 11.

Where we are at the end of Module 1

By the end of 2002 Australia has: a Muslim population of roughly a quarter of a million, concentrated in a few suburbs of two cities; a small ideological fringe with foreign contacts and no serious operational capacity; one convicted conspirator; 88 dead citizens in Bali; and, for the first time, a body of law that makes preparation itself a crime.

What it does not yet have is a home-grown network willing and able to attack Australia. That arrives within twelve months.

Module Two

The First Wave

Willie Brigitte, Faheem Lodhi, and the largest counter-terrorism operation Australia had ever mounted.

2001–2006 12 min Operation Pendennis

In May 2003 a heavyset Frenchman from Guadeloupe stepped off a plane at Sydney Airport on a tourist visa. He spoke little English. He had been trained by a Pakistani militant group in a camp near Muzaffarabad. Within days of arriving he had married an Australian woman he barely knew, moved into a flat in Lakemba, and begun asking his contacts about chemicals, detonators and the layout of Australian military bases.

Nobody in Australia knew who he was. The French did.

Willie Brigitte

Willie Virgile Brigitte was born in Guadeloupe in 1968 and converted to Islam in Paris in his twenties. By the late 1990s he was moving in the circles that French intelligence watched most closely: veterans and aspirants around the Paris mosques, some of whom had been to Afghanistan. In 2001 he travelled to Pakistan and trained with Lashkar-e-Taiba.

Lashkar-e-Taiba

"The Army of the Pure": a Pakistani jihadist organisation founded in the late 1980s, focused primarily on Kashmir and backed for years by elements of the Pakistani state. It ran large, well-organised training camps and, unusually, was willing to train Westerners. It was responsible for the 2008 Mumbai attacks. Its camps are the connective tissue of the early Australian cases — several of the men in this module passed through them.

Brigitte came to Australia because Lashkar sent him. His job was to establish himself, link up with a local facilitator, and prepare an operation. The facilitator was already in place.

Lodhi was, on paper, the last person you would expect. He was a qualified architect, married, respectable, in his mid-thirties, working for a Sydney practice. He had come to Australia in 1998. In 2001 he went to Pakistan and trained at a Lashkar camp; when he came back he began, quietly and methodically, to build the components of an attack.

The target was never fixed — and this became the central legal question in his trial. The Crown could not say what Lodhi had decided to blow up. What it could show was that he had assembled, over months, precisely the things a person would need in order to decide: the maps, the aerial photographs, the chemical enquiries made under an assumed name to a Melbourne supplier, the manual. Under the 2002 legislation, that was enough. Preparation was now the offence.

Brigitte was arrested in October 2003, held under Australia's immigration powers, and deported to France within days — a decision that spared Australia a difficult trial and that was criticised at the time as an evasion. He was later convicted in France and sentenced to nine years. Lodhi was arrested in April 2004 and tried in the New South Wales Supreme Court in 2006.

Lodhi's case established the shape of Australian terrorism prosecution: you do not need to prove what they were going to do, only that they were getting ready to do something.

That principle has done an enormous amount of work in the twenty years since. It is also the reason Australia's terrorism convictions are so often for offences that sound abstract — "doing an act in preparation for a terrorist act", "possessing a thing connected with the preparation of a terrorist act" — and why sentences that look severe are attached to conduct that never produced a casualty. Supporters of the regime say this is the entire point: you cannot wait. Critics say it criminalises intention and drags in the reckless, the fantasising and the merely angry alongside the genuinely dangerous. Both are partly right.

Khazaal's case matters because it was about words. He built nothing and attacked no one. The prosecution was for authorship and publication. That the conviction stood — it survived a High Court challenge in 2012 — established that in Australia the production of jihadist instructional material is itself a serious criminal offence, a principle that would be applied hundreds of times in the Islamic State years.

Operation Pendennis

In 2004, ASIO and police in Melbourne and Sydney began watching two groups of men who appeared to be in contact with each other. The investigation that followed — codenamed Pendennis, run jointly by Victoria Police, New South Wales Police, the Australian Federal Police and ASIO — became the largest counter-terrorism operation in Australian history to that point and produced the country's first significant home-grown terrorist convictions.

It ran for eighteen months. Investigators installed listening devices in cars, homes and a Melbourne prayer room and accumulated something in the order of tens of thousands of hours of recordings. What they heard was a small, disorganised, intensely committed group talking themselves steadily towards mass murder.

Benbrika had arrived in Australia in 1989 on a tourist visa, overstayed, married an Australian citizen, and become a citizen himself. He worked intermittently, studied theology by correspondence, and by the early 2000s had gathered a following among young men who found the established Melbourne mosques dull and compromised. He was, on the surveillance recordings, not a commanding figure — he was querulous, repetitive and often confused about basic facts — but he had absolute authority over the men around him, and he used it.

The recordings are the heart of the case. On them, Benbrika talks about the need for "maximum damage", about killing a thousand people, about the difference between an operation that would merely irritate the government and one that would force it out of Iraq. His followers went bush near Kyneton and near Mount Disappointment to practise with firearms and to bond. They stockpiled chemicals, laboratory glassware and batteries. They collected instructional material.

Six of Benbrika's Melbourne followers were convicted alongside him and received sentences ranging from about four years to seven and a half. Others — including a convert named Shane Kent — were convicted on narrower charges or pleaded guilty. Several men were acquitted outright, a point worth holding onto: these were contested trials with real defences, and juries did not simply convict everyone put in front of them.

The Sydney cell

The Sydney group was, if anything, further advanced. Its members were mostly of Lebanese background and mostly from the south-west; several had trained abroad. Over 2004 and 2005 they acquired firearms, ammunition and about 130 litres of chemicals capable of producing explosives, and they too went to a rural property — near Bourke, in far western New South Wales — to shoot.

The Sydney sentences — the longest terrorism sentences imposed in Australia at that time — reflected how far the conspiracy had gone. But they also reflected a judgement about deterrence, and about the difficulty of assessing the risk posed by men who had not yet chosen a target. Appeals against both the Melbourne and Sydney convictions were largely unsuccessful.

The law follows the case

Pendennis had one further consequence, and it is an uncomfortable one. In late 2005, with the operation nearing its end, the Howard government pushed an urgent amendment through Parliament changing the wording of the terrorism offences from planning "the" terrorist act to planning "a" terrorist act — that is, removing any requirement that a specific target be identified. The then Premier of New South Wales publicly linked the amendment to a live operation. Arrests followed within days.

Whether that was responsible government moving quickly to close a gap, or the legislature retro-fitting the law around a prosecution it wanted to succeed, was argued at the time and has been argued since. It is a good early example of a pattern that recurs throughout this story: in Australia, terrorism law has usually been made in the immediate aftermath of an event, at speed, with bipartisan support and limited scrutiny.

What the first wave was, and wasn't

None of these men killed anybody in Australia. Every one of the plots in this module was interdicted before it produced a casualty. That is a genuine achievement of Australian policing and intelligence, and it is worth saying plainly, because the counter-terrorism record is usually described only through its failures.

But it also means that the entire first wave is a story about intentions and preparations, assessed by courts on the balance of recordings and objects. Reasonable people disagreed then and disagree now about how close some of these men actually were to acting.

What the first wave left behind

By 2010 the men of Pendennis were in prison, most of them for a very long time. The Melbourne scene around Benbrika dispersed. The Sydney networks did not.

Three things carried forward. The first was kinship: the Sydney cell was built out of families — uncles and nephews, brothers, brothers-in-law — and those families did not go away. Mohamed Ali Elomar's nephews would surface a decade later, one of them in Syria. The second was prison: a cohort of men were now serving long sentences together in the high-security units at Goulburn and Barwon, where they would meet the next generation. The third was grievance: the raids of 2005 were experienced across parts of south-west Sydney not as a narrow police operation but as a collective accusation, and that feeling was carefully cultivated by people who had no intention of ever breaking the law themselves.

Which brings us to the milieu.

Module Three

The Milieu, Part One

Suburbs, mosques, bookshops and preachers — the lawful world that the unlawful one grew inside.

1995–2015 12 min Read the caution
Before you read this module

This module names people who have never been charged with, let alone convicted of, any terrorism offence. Several of them have been the subject of sustained and sometimes unfair media attention. Saying that a person's teaching or organisation forms part of the environment in which violent extremism developed is not the same as saying that person committed a crime, endorsed a specific attack, or is legally responsible for anything anyone else did.

Where someone has been found by a court to have breached a law, that is stated. Where they have not, that is stated too. Please read it in that spirit.

Terrorism does not emerge from nowhere and it does not, in practice, emerge from individuals sitting alone in rooms — or at least it did not for the first twenty years of this story. It emerges from what researchers call a radical milieu: a wider circle of people who share the worldview, admire the fighters, supply the vocabulary and the friendships and the sense of significance, and who do not themselves break the law.

The milieu is not a conspiracy. It is a social environment. Its most important property is that it makes an extraordinary idea — that you might one day kill strangers — seem ordinary, discussable, even admirable, to a small number of the people passing through it.

Geography

Two clusters account for the great majority of Australian jihadist cases.

The first is south-western Sydney: the corridor running through Lakemba, Wiley Park, Punchbowl, Bankstown, Greenacre, Yagoona and Auburn, with outliers at Liverpool, Minto and Guildford. This is the heartland of Australia's Lebanese Muslim community, settled from the mid-1970s. It is dense, young, kin-based and, in parts, poor. Nearly all of the Pendennis Sydney cell came from within a few kilometres of one another.

The second is Melbourne's northern and south-eastern suburbs: Preston, Coburg, Fawkner, Broadmeadows, Meadow Heights and Dallas in the north; Springvale, Dandenong and Endeavour Hills in the south-east. The northern suburbs are historically Turkish and Lebanese; the south-eastern belt is more mixed, with substantial Afghan, Bosnian and later Somali populations.

Nothing about these places causes terrorism. Hundreds of thousands of people have lived unremarkable lives in them. But density matters: when a preacher opens a prayer room in Springvale South, everyone in a certain age bracket within ten kilometres hears about it within a week.

Institutions

Ahlus Sunnah wal Jamaah, Melbourne

The most established Salafi institution in Australia is the Ahlus Sunnah wal Jamaah Association, long associated with Sheikh Mohammed Omran, a Jordanian-born cleric who has lived in Melbourne since the 1980s and preaches from Brunswick. Omran is a serious and doctrinally conservative figure with real scholarly standing in Salafi circles. He has also made statements that made him a fixture of tabloid coverage — most famously, in 2005, appearing to dispute that al-Qaeda existed as an organisation and defending Osama bin Laden as a good Muslim.

Omran has never been charged with any offence. He has publicly opposed terrorism in Australia, has said repeatedly that violence here is forbidden, and has co-operated with authorities on occasion. He also, and this is the point, taught and hosted young men who later went in directions he did not sanction — Abdul Nacer Benbrika was in his orbit before the two fell out and Benbrika set up on his own. That pattern — a mainstream-conservative teacher, a splinter, a hardening — recurs constantly.

Al-Furqan Islamic Information Centre, Springvale South

Al-Furqan was a prayer room and study centre in Melbourne's south-east run by Harun Mehicevic, a Bosnian-born preacher who arrived in Australia in the 1990s. It ran classes, sold and distributed literature, and drew a young, mostly Australian-born crowd.

Its alumni list is, in retrospect, extraordinary. Numan Haider, who stabbed two police officers in 2014, attended. So did Sevdet Besim and Harun Causevic, arrested over the 2015 Anzac Day plot. So did Neil Prakash, who became the Islamic State's most prominent Australian recruiter. The centre was raided in 2012 and again in 2015, and closed its doors in April 2015, saying it had been driven out by "constant harassment, pressure and false accusations".

Mehicevic has never been charged with a terrorism offence, and the centre consistently denied any connection to the plots for which its former attendees were later convicted. Both things are true at once: no crime was ever established against the institution, and a remarkable share of Victoria's Islamic State cohort passed through its door.

The bookshop problem

Small independent Islamic bookshops and prayer rooms recur throughout this history — in Melbourne, in Sydney, in Perth. They are cheap to open, require no accreditation, answer to no council of elders, and can be run by a charismatic man with no formal training whatsoever. Established mosques have boards, imams with credentials, insurance policies and a strong institutional interest in not attracting police attention. A shopfront has none of that. It is the classic weak point in a decentralised religion.

A charismatic preacher, trained in Medina, who ran a youth centre in Liverpool from 2000 and built a large following among young men in Sydney's south-west. Campaigns for the restoration of a global caliphate, rejects democracy and electoral participation as un-Islamic, and refuses to condemn attacks on Western targets abroad — while maintaining, as a matter of doctrine, that it does not itself engage in armed struggle. It holds public conferences, runs street stalls and produces a great deal of material.

Its statements have caused repeated controversy: al-Wahwah has made statements about Jews that critics characterise as antisemitic and Holocaust-minimising; Badar confirmed in 2017 that the group holds apostasy to attract capital punishment under Islamic law; a talk Badar was scheduled to give in 2014 titled "Honour Killings Are Morally Justified" was cancelled by the Festival of Dangerous Ideas after an outcry, and Badar said the title was not his and misrepresented his argument.

The organisation is not proscribed in Australia and none of these individuals has been charged with a terrorism offence. Successive governments — Coalition and Labor — examined banning it and concluded the legal test was not met, since proscription requires a finding that an organisation directly or indirectly engages in, prepares, plans, assists or fosters the doing of a terrorist act.

Hizb ut-Tahrir occupies the single most contested position in this landscape. What is not seriously disputable is that a number of men later convicted of terrorism offences in Australia had passed through Hizb ut-Tahrir events on their way, and that the organisation regards this as no more its responsibility than a university is responsible for its dropouts.

The uncomfortable fact at the centre of this module: nearly everything that made the difference was legal.

The conflation problem

It needs saying that Australian public debate in this period was very bad at distinguishing between categories, and this did real damage.

In 2006, the Grand Mufti of Australia, Sheikh Taj el-Din al-Hilaly, delivered a Ramadan sermon in which he compared immodestly dressed women to uncovered meat left out for cats. It was a repellent thing to say and it was covered for weeks. Hilaly was not a jihadist, had no connection to any plot, and had in fact been publicly critical of extremism; he was a socially conservative cleric with a talent for saying inflammatory things. But in the coverage he became simply "the radical cleric", indistinguishable from Feiz Mohammad, who was indistinguishable from Benbrika, who was an actual convicted terrorist.

The cost of that flattening was twofold. It made the Muslim community defensive and suspicious of a media it reasonably believed would not distinguish between a boorish sermon and a bomb plot — which made co-operation harder. And it fed a counter-narrative, energetically promoted inside the milieu, that Australia was engaged in a general campaign against Islam and that any Muslim who co-operated with police was a traitor.

Cronulla, December 2005

A month after the Pendennis arrests, several thousand people gathered at Cronulla beach in Sydney's south, many of them drunk, some carrying signs reading "we grew here, you flew here", and attacked people they took to be of Middle Eastern appearance. Convoys of young men from the south-west drove to the beachside suburbs that night and the next and smashed cars and shopfronts in reprisal.

Cronulla was not a jihadist event and had no organisational connection to anything in this guide. It matters for two reasons. It was the most vivid demonstration in modern Australian history that the ethnic fault line running through Sydney could produce mass violence. And it entered the milieu's story about itself — proof, endlessly recirculated in sermons and pamphlets and later on Telegram, that Australians would turn on Muslims given any excuse. Twenty years later, in the days after the Bondi Beach attack, flyers calling for a repeat of Cronulla circulated on social media, and New South Wales Police banned public assemblies across the entire Sydney metropolitan area for a fortnight to prevent it.

The other side of the ledger

It would be a serious distortion to end this module without saying that the milieu was, throughout, a minority position within a minority.

The Australian National Imams Council, the Lebanese Muslim Association, the great majority of mosque boards and essentially every recognised scholarly body in the country condemned al-Qaeda, condemned Bali, condemned the Islamic State when it came, and did so repeatedly and publicly. Muslim families reported their own children to police — in several cases in this guide, the first tip-off came from inside the household. Muslim community workers ran intervention programs, often with very little support and considerable abuse from both directions. In December 2025 a Syrian-born Muslim man named Ahmed al-Ahmed ran at an armed terrorist with his bare hands at Bondi Beach and took his rifle off him.

The milieu was real. So was the resistance to it. Any account that reports only one is not describing Australia.

Module Four

The Quiet Years

Holsworthy, the Somali networks, prison, and the road that opened to Syria.

2007–2013 13 min Operation Neath

For most of the period between the Pendennis arrests and the rise of the Islamic State, Australian counter-terrorism looked like a solved problem. The plotters were in prison. The legislation was in place. Al-Qaeda was being dismantled by drones in Waziristan. The annual reports grew calmer.

Two things were happening underneath that were not visible from the annual reports. A different diaspora was producing a different kind of plot. And a war was about to start that would pull more Australians into jihadist organisations than every previous conflict combined.

The Somali networks

Somalia's civil war produced a substantial refugee community in Melbourne, concentrated in the northern suburbs, and a smaller one in Sydney and Perth. Most arrived in the 1990s with nothing, into a country whose labour market had no obvious place for them, and the community's outcomes on almost every social measure were poor.

From 2006, Somalia was convulsed again. An alliance of sharia courts briefly took Mogadishu; Ethiopia invaded with American backing and drove them out; and out of the wreckage came al-Shabaab — "the Youth" — which fought a long insurgency and eventually pledged allegiance to al-Qaeda. Al-Shabaab was unusually effective at recruiting from the Somali diaspora in the West, and it recruited in Australia.

In 2007 the Australian Federal Police opened Operation Rochester into reports that Somali-Australians were travelling to fight. In February 2009 a second investigation, Operation Neath, began looking at domestic support for the group. Neath found something rather worse than fundraising.

The Holsworthy Barracks plot

The plan

A group of men in Melbourne intended to enter Holsworthy Barracks, a large Australian Army base in Sydney's south-west, with automatic weapons and shoot as many soldiers and others as they could until they were killed or captured. It was, in modern terminology, a fedayeen-style armed assault: no escape plan, no demands, maximum casualties.

They sought religious sanction for it, sending an emissary to Somalia to ask senior al-Shabaab-aligned clerics for a fatwa authorising an attack in Australia. No such authorisation was ever obtained.

And its acquittals demonstrate the sharp edge of the preparatory-offence regime: a jury that has heard the same intercepts can reasonably conclude that one man was in and another was merely nearby.

Prison

By 2010, Australia had accumulated something it had never had before: a population of convicted terrorists serving long sentences, most of them in the high-security units at Goulburn in New South Wales — including the facility universally known as Supermax — and at Barwon in Victoria.

Prison authorities faced a genuine dilemma with no clean answer. Concentrate them, and you create a seminary. Disperse them, and you seed the general population. Australia has mostly chosen concentration, with segregation regimes of extraordinary severity for the highest-risk prisoners. The evidence on whether this works is contested and thin.

What is not contested is that prison changed some of these men, and not for the better. Khaled Sharrouf is the clearest case. He had pleaded guilty to a minor Pendennis charge, served under four years, and been released in 2009 with a documented history of serious mental illness. He came out more committed than he went in, and in December 2013 he left Australia for Syria on his brother's passport.

A structural feature, not an anecdote

Look for this pattern throughout the rest of this guide: a man is convicted of a lesser offence, serves a short sentence, is released, and reoffends far more seriously. Sharrouf. Yacqub Khayre. Man Haron Monis was on bail. The Bondi attacker's family associations had been examined by ASIO six years before the attack and assessed as low risk. The single most consistent criticism of the Australian system across two decades is not that it fails to detect people. It is that it detects them, files them, and then loses interest.

Hyde Park, September 2012

On 15 September 2012, several hundred people gathered in Sydney's Hyde Park to protest against Innocence of Muslims, a crude anti-Islamic video made in California that had provoked demonstrations across the Muslim world. The protest turned violent. Police were attacked with bottles and sticks; officers used pepper spray and dogs; eight officers and several protesters were injured.

The image that defined it was a small child holding a placard reading "behead all those who insult the prophet".

Among those convicted of offences arising from the riot was Ahmed Elomar, a former boxer who struck a police officer with a flagpole; he received a sentence of around two and a half years for assault occasioning actual bodily harm. He was not charged with any terrorism offence. His brother Mohamed Elomar left for Syria the following year and became one of the two most notorious Australians in the Islamic State.

Hyde Park was not terrorism. It was a riot. But it was the moment at which a mostly invisible milieu became visible to the general public, and it was read inside that milieu as a proof of strength. Several men later convicted of Islamic State–related offences were present.

Syria

In March 2011, protests against the Assad government began in Deraa. By 2012 Syria was in full civil war. By 2013 large parts of the north and east were controlled by armed opposition groups, and the most effective of those groups were increasingly Islamist.

For Australians in the milieu, Syria was different from everything that had come before, in four ways.

It was morally legible. Bali was mass murder of tourists. Holsworthy was a plan to shoot soldiers in their beds. Syria was a dictator barrel-bombing his own cities, filmed and uploaded daily. A young man in Bankstown who would have recoiled from al-Qaeda could tell himself, plausibly and sometimes sincerely, that he was going to protect civilians.

It was reachable. Fly to Istanbul on a tourist visa, take a domestic flight to Gaziantep or Hatay, get in a car. No visa, no vetting, no camp in Waziristan. The journey could be done in two days by anyone with a passport and three thousand dollars.

It had a support scene at home. Legitimate Syrian aid convoys and fundraising drives ran alongside, and sometimes overlapped with, recruitment. Distinguishing between the two was extremely difficult for authorities and for donors alike, and a number of Australians who genuinely thought they were funding food ended up funding something else.

It came with a media operation. The groups in Syria were the first jihadist organisations to be natively fluent in social media. Recruitment did not require a preacher in a garage; it required Twitter, and later Telegram, and a friend already there.

A note on numbers

Official Australian estimates of foreign-fighter travel shifted repeatedly and were never precise. The figures most often cited are that around 230 Australians travelled to Syria and Iraq to join jihadist groups over the whole period; that roughly 100 or more were killed there; and that around 40 returned. Several hundred more were assessed as providing support from within Australia, and around 200 passports were cancelled to prevent travel. Treat all of these as orders of magnitude rather than counts.

Proportionate to population, Australia's contribution to the Islamic State's foreign contingent was among the highest in the English-speaking world.

What the state was doing while this happened

It is worth pausing on the institutional picture, because the years 2007 to 2013 are when Australia's counter-terrorism machinery took the shape it still has.

The Joint Counter Terrorism Teams — standing partnerships between the Australian Federal Police, ASIO and each state police force — were established from 2002 and matured in this period. They are the reason almost every operation in this guide has a single codename despite involving four or five agencies across two jurisdictions, and by international standards they work unusually well. Britain and the United States have both studied the model.

ASIO's budget and staffing roughly tripled between 2001 and 2010, and it moved into a purpose-built headquarters in Canberra. The Independent National Security Legislation Monitor was created in 2010 to review the growing body of legislation, modelled in part on the United Kingdom's Independent Reviewer of Terrorism Legislation. It has produced some of the sharpest criticism of these laws anywhere, and governments have frequently ignored it.

At the same time the state was quietly discovering the limits of what it had built. The 2010 Council of Australian Governments review of counter-terrorism laws found a great deal of it unused: control orders had been issued twice in five years, preventative detention orders never. The powers had been legislated in the heat of 2005 and then sat on the shelf, which is either reassuring or an indictment depending on your view of whether laws should be passed that nobody intends to use.

The other quiet development was the beginning of Australia's countering violent extremism effort — community grants, intervention programs, funding for youth work in the suburbs named in Module 3. It was small, it was under-evaluated, and it suffered from the near-fatal branding problem that a program aimed at preventing terrorism among Muslim youth announces to Muslim youth that they are suspected of terrorism. Practitioners have been saying so since 2010.

The cases nobody remembers

The impression of a lull is partly an artefact of memory. Between 2010 and 2013 there were arrests, charges and convictions — they were simply smaller and less spectacular. Men were prosecuted for funding al-Shabaab. Others were charged over attempts to travel. A Sydney man was convicted of possessing extremist material. The system was ticking over.

Two things about that period are worth carrying forward. First, the offences being charged were increasingly at the low end — possession, funding, attempted travel — which is what a preventive regime looks like when it is functioning: it catches people early and cheaply. Second, several of the people charged in these years reappear later in this guide with much more serious charges, which is what a preventive regime looks like when it is failing: it catches people early, imposes a short sentence, and returns them to the same networks.

The quiet years were not quiet. They were the period in which the next decade was assembled: a cohort radicalised in the 2000s and released from prison; a new diaspora with a new war; a riot that taught the milieu it had numbers; a state apparatus that had grown enormously without ever being seriously tested; and, finally, an open road to a battlefield that would give several hundred Australians the training, the contacts and the authority they had never had before.

Module Five

Exodus

Australians in the Islamic State, 2013–2019: the fighters, the recruiters, the propagandists, the wives and the children.

In August 2014 a photograph appeared on Twitter. It showed a small boy, seven years old, in a baseball cap, holding a severed human head in both hands because it was too heavy for one. The doctor, and the ordinary ones

Not everyone who went was a fighter, and the range of people who did is the most under-reported part of this story.

Tareq Kamleh was a paediatric registrar from Adelaide, born in Australia to a Palestinian-Jordanian father, well liked by colleagues, with no history of religious observance that anyone around him noticed. In April 2015 he appeared in an Islamic State propaganda video, filmed in a hospital in Raqqa under the name Abu Yusuf al-Australi, calmly appealing to Western medical professionals to come and staff the caliphate's health system. He was presumed killed in the fall of Raqqa in 2017; he was in fact found alive years later in an Iraqi prison.

The Islamic State recruited administrators, engineers, media producers, teachers and doctors, and it did so with a pitch aimed squarely at educated professionals: not "come and fight" but "come and build". That pitch worked on a number of Australians who fit no profile anyone was screening for.

The rest were mostly unremarkable young men from the suburbs named in Module 3 — tradesmen, security guards, unemployed school-leavers — who went in groups of two and three, followed friends, and were used as infantry. Australian officials noted early on that the Islamic State tended to deploy Western recruits as shock troops or suicide bombers rather than as skilled operators, on the reasonable ground that they were expendable, enthusiastic and useless at Arabic. The mortality rate reflects it.

The women and the children

Somewhere in the order of forty to sixty Australian women travelled to Islamic State territory, some as wives accompanying husbands, some independently in response to a specific and well-organised female recruitment effort. Zehra Duman, a young woman from Melbourne, ran one of the more prominent English-language accounts encouraging others to follow. Amira Karroum, from the Gold Coast, was killed in Syria within weeks of arriving in January 2014.

The children are the hardest part of this story and the part on which Australian policy has been least coherent.

When the caliphate collapsed in 2019, tens of thousands of women and children associated with Islamic State fighters ended up in Kurdish-run camps in north-eastern Syria — principally al-Hawl and the smaller al-Roj. Among them were several dozen Australians, most of them children, many of them born there, some of them orphans.

The argument, briefly

For repatriation: they are Australian citizens; most are children who chose nothing; leaving them in camps run by a non-state actor in a collapsing region guarantees a new generation raised inside the ideology, beyond any Australian jurisdiction; and returnees brought home under supervision can be prosecuted, monitored and, where possible, reintegrated.

Against: the adults made a choice to join a genocidal organisation; security agencies cannot reliably assess who among them remains committed; the resources required to monitor each returnee are enormous and come at the expense of other work; and no government wants to explain a returnee's attack to the public.

Both arguments are made in good faith by serious people. Australia has, in practice, done a little of the first and mostly the second.

Citizenship

From 2015, Australia gave itself the power to strip citizenship from dual nationals involved in terrorism — initially operating automatically by force of law, later by ministerial decision. Around twenty people lost their citizenship under these provisions.

The courts then took most of it apart. In 2022 the High Court struck down the ministerial power in Alexander, holding that deciding whether a person had committed conduct serious enough to forfeit citizenship was a judicial function, not a ministerial one. In 2023, in Benbrika's case, the High Court held that his citizenship had been invalidly cancelled and restored it. Prakash's cancellation collapsed on the factual point that Fiji did not regard him as a citizen and Australia cannot render a person stateless.

The policy has since been rebuilt to operate through the courts on sentencing. The episode is a good illustration of a recurring dynamic: Parliament legislates fast and broadly after an outrage; the High Court, some years later, finds the constitutional limit; and the policy is rebuilt in narrower form with much less public attention.

By 2019 the caliphate was gone. The Australians who had gone to it were dead, imprisoned abroad, stranded in camps, or — a small number — back home. What did not disappear was the audience.

That audience is the subject of the second half of this guide. The organisation that had commanded it lost its territory, its treasury and its ability to direct anything. What it retained was a doctrine, refined from 2014 onwards and repeated in every issue of its newspaper, that its supporters should stop trying to travel and start killing where they stood.

Module Six

The Home Front

Fifteen months in which Australia experienced its first jihadist killings on home soil.

Sept 2014 – Dec 2015 13 min Lindt Café · Parramatta

On 22 September 2014, an Islamic State spokesman named Abu Muhammad al-Adnani released an audio statement. It told supporters everywhere to stop trying to reach Syria and instead to kill non-believers where they lived — "smash his head with a rock, or slaughter him with a knife, or run him over with your car". It named Australia in the first sentence, alongside America and France.

Almost nothing in Australian counter-terrorism after that date makes sense without it. Adnani's statement converted a hierarchical organisation into an open franchise. It required no travel, no training, no contact and no permission. Australia found out what that meant within twenty-four hours.

The New South Wales State Coroner, Michael Barnes, found that the siege was a terrorist incident and that Monis was solely responsible for both deaths.

He was scathing about the Director of Public Prosecutions' handling of Monis's bail: under New South Wales law as it then stood, the DPP could have applied to revoke it at any time and did not, despite Monis publicly posting threats against Australia's allies days before.

The argument about Monis

More ink has been spilled on whether Man Haron Monis was "really" a terrorist than on any other case in this guide, and the argument is worth understanding because it recurs.

The case for no: Monis was a chaotic, grandiose, litigious fantasist with a long history of violence against women and no connection whatsoever to any organisation. He had been a Shia and became a Sunni conveniently late. He made no martyrdom video, left no manifesto, and by the coroner's own finding made little sophisticated preparation. A published analysis in a forensic psychiatry journal argued directly that this was not a terrorist attack but the final act of a disturbed and dangerous individual who reached for the most powerful available script.

The case for yes: he pledged allegiance to the Islamic State, demanded its flag, framed his demands politically, targeted a public space in the heart of a national financial centre, and did all of it within twelve weeks of a public instruction to do exactly that. The coroner, having heard everything, found it was terrorism.

The most useful conclusion is that the question is badly framed. Severe personality disorder, grievance, misogyny and ideology are not competing explanations. In a substantial share of the attacks in this guide — and in the overwhelming majority of the lone-actor cases after 2016 — they are all present at once, and picking one to be the "real" cause tells you more about the analyst than the attacker.

Australia's first jihadist mass-casualty siege was carried out by a man who was, at that moment, out on bail for accessory to murder and forty sexual assaults. That is the single most important sentence in the whole Lindt Café story.

The consequences were substantial: a joint Commonwealth–New South Wales review, a national review of siege response, wholesale reform of New South Wales bail law to create a presumption against bail for terrorism-linked offences, and a permanent change in Australian police doctrine towards early intervention in active-armed-offender situations rather than containment.

Module Seven

The Grind

Minto, Melbourne, Brighton — and the most sophisticated terrorist plot ever mounted in Australia.

2016–2019 12 min Operation Silves

The years after 2015 have no single defining event. They are a grind: a steady rhythm of attempted attacks, most of them small, most of them disrupted, punctuated by two that were not. It is the least remembered part of this story and in some ways the most instructive, because it shows what a mature domestic jihadist threat actually looks like when it is neither growing nor going away.

Minto, September 2016

On a Saturday afternoon in September 2016, a 22-year-old man named Ihsas Khan walked out of his house in Minto, in Sydney's south-west, carrying a hunting knife, and attacked the first person he came across — a 59-year-old neighbour, Wayne Greenhalgh, who was walking his dog. Greenhalgh was stabbed repeatedly and seriously injured; he survived because a nearby shopkeeper intervened and locked Khan out of his premises.

Khan told police afterwards that he had done it to avenge Australian and American military action in Syria and Iraq, and to force a change in Australian policy. He had no accomplices, no organisational contact, and had self-radicalised primarily online. He was convicted in 2018 of committing a terrorist act and sentenced to 36 years' imprisonment with a non-parole period of 27.

The sentence — for an attack in which nobody died — caused comment at the time. It reflects a deliberate sentencing policy in New South Wales for completed terrorist acts, and it sits oddly beside the far shorter sentences given for equivalent violence without an ideological element. Whether that is proportionate is a live question in Australian criminal law.

Melbourne, Christmas 2016

In the week before Christmas 2016, some 400 Victorian and federal police executed warrants across Melbourne's north-west in Operation Kastelholm. What they interrupted was the most advanced multi-target plot Australia had seen since Pendennis.

The plan

Explosives, knives and, if obtainable, a firearm, deployed on Christmas Day against Federation Square, Flinders Street Station and St Paul's Cathedral — three of the busiest and most symbolically loaded sites in central Melbourne, within a few hundred metres of each other.

Police seized machetes, partly constructed improvised explosive devices, and devices containing Islamic State propaganda and bomb-making instructions.

Three men — Abdullah Chaarani, Ahmed Mohamed and Hamza Abbas — were convicted in October 2018 of conspiring to do acts in preparation for a terrorist act, after an earlier trial ended without a verdict. In November 2019 Abbas was sentenced to 22 years and Chaarani and Mohamed to 26 years each.

Chaarani and Mohamed were already serving sentences for something else: the arson of the Imam Ali Islamic Centre in Fawkner, a Shia mosque, in December 2016. With cumulation, their effective sentences ran to 38 years apiece.

That detail deserves attention. Australian jihadists have attacked Muslims as well as non-Muslims — a Shia mosque in Melbourne, a planned bombing of a Shia prayer hall in Sydney. Salafi-jihadism regards Shia Islam as apostasy, and the sectarian dimension of the ideology is routinely missed in Australian coverage, which tends to frame everything as Muslims versus the West.

Brighton, June 2017

On the evening of 5 June 2017, a 29-year-old man walked into the foyer of a serviced apartment building in Brighton, an affluent bayside suburb of Melbourne, and shot dead the receptionist, Kai Hao, a 36-year-old newly married father. He then took a woman hostage in an apartment and telephoned a television newsroom to say he was acting for the Islamic State and al-Qaeda. When the police tactical unit arrived he came out shooting, wounded three officers, and was killed.

The gunman was Yacqub Khayre — the same Yacqub Khayre who had been acquitted by a jury in 2010 over the Holsworthy Barracks plot.

Why Brighton is so difficult

Khayre had a long record of serious violence, drug offences and burglary. He had been in and out of custody. He was on parole at the time. A serious argument can be made that his real problem was violent criminality and addiction, and that the Islamic State claim — which the group duly made — was an afterthought reaching for a bigger stage.

A serious argument can also be made that he had been in and around jihadist networks for a decade and knew exactly what he was doing.

The political consequence was concrete and immediate: parole reform. Victoria and other states substantially tightened parole for offenders with any terrorism connection. Whether that was the right lesson to draw from a case whose ideological content is genuinely ambiguous is still argued.

Operation Silves: the plane

Everything else in this module was, in the end, men with knives. This was different.

In April 2017, a senior Islamic State commander in Syria named Tarek Khayat made contact with two of his brothers in Sydney. The family was from Lakemba. Khaled Khayat was 49; Mahmoud Khayat was 32.

Over the following months, components of a functioning improvised explosive device — including military-grade explosive — were air-freighted from Turkey to Sydney, concealed in a consignment. Under instruction transmitted by encrypted messaging, the brothers assembled the device inside the body of a domestic meat grinder, which was in turn to be packed inside a suitcase.

The suitcase was to be carried aboard Etihad flight EY451 from Sydney to Abu Dhabi on 15 July 2017, carrying around 400 people. It was to detonate roughly twenty minutes after take-off, over water.

The man chosen to carry it was Amer Khayat — another brother, who had no idea what was in the bag.

A man was to be handed a suitcase by his own brothers, told it was a gift for relatives, and put on a plane with four hundred people to be blown apart over the Indian Ocean.

The plot failed at the check-in counter. The bag was overweight. Khaled Khayat took it back, and the brothers left the airport; Amer flew to Lebanon without it. The device was later disassembled and hidden.

Undeterred, the brothers moved to a second plan: a chemical dispersal device intended to release hydrogen sulphide gas in an enclosed public space — a train carriage was among the possibilities discussed. They had begun acquiring precursors when, on 26 July 2017, a foreign intelligence partner alerted Australian authorities that a plot existed. The New South Wales Joint Counter Terrorism Team opened Operation Silves. Arrests followed on 29 July, three days later.

Outcome

Khaled Khayat — 40 years' imprisonment, non-parole period 30 years.
Mahmoud Khayat — 36 years, non-parole period 27 years.

Both were convicted in 2019. Amer Khayat, the unwitting courier, spent more than two years in Roumieh prison in Lebanon and was acquitted by a Lebanese military court within hours of his brother's conviction in Sydney. Tarek Khayat, the brother in Syria who designed the plot, was sentenced in absentia in Lebanon.

Silves matters more than any other disrupted plot in this guide, for three reasons.

It was directed, not inspired. An Islamic State external operations cell in Syria supplied the design, the explosive and the instructions, using a family relationship for trust. This was the same methodology used in plots in Europe and South-East Asia in the same period.

It progressed undetected for six months in a country with some of the most extensive counter-terrorism powers in the democratic world. It was not found by Australian agencies. It was handed to them by a foreign partner, three days before they moved.

It nearly worked. Had the suitcase weighed two kilograms less, the deadliest terrorist attack in Australian history would have occurred in July 2017, and it would have been an aviation bombing.

Bourke Street, November 2018

On the afternoon of 9 November 2018, a man drove a utility loaded with gas cylinders into Bourke Street in central Melbourne and set it alight. The cylinders did not explode. He then attacked passers-by with a knife.

Sisto Malaspina, the 74-year-old co-owner of Pellegrini's Espresso Bar and one of the most recognisable figures in Melbourne's Italian community, was fatally stabbed. Two others were wounded. The attacker was shot by police and died in hospital. He was Hassan Khalif Shire Ali, 30, who had come to Australia from Somalia as a child. His passport had been cancelled in 2015 after he tried to travel to Syria.

The whole attack lasted nineteen seconds. The coroner found in 2021 that it was motivated by adherence to Islamist extremism and the Islamic State, while noting a documented history of drug use and mental illness. Shire Ali's brother had been arrested the previous year over a plan to attack Federation Square on New Year's Eve, and was subsequently convicted.

Melbourne's response — a state funeral for a café owner, and the crowds that turned out for it — became a minor national event in itself.

Christchurch

15 March 2019

On a Friday afternoon in Christchurch, New Zealand, an Australian citizen from Grafton, New South Wales, walked into the Al Noor Mosque and the Linwood Islamic Centre and shot dead 51 Muslim worshippers, livestreaming as he went. He was motivated by white nationalist and "great replacement" ideology and was sentenced to life without parole.

It is the deadliest act of terrorism ever committed by an Australian, anywhere.

Christchurch does not belong to the history of Australian jihadism, but no honest account of that history can leave it out, for two reasons.

First, it fundamentally reordered Australian counter-terrorism. Within eighteen months, ASIO was publicly stating that right-wing and nationalist violent extremism accounted for a large and growing share of its priority caseload — at points approaching forty per cent. The agency retired the term "Islamic extremism" from its public vocabulary altogether in 2021, replacing it with the ideology-neutral categories "religiously motivated violent extremism" and "nationalist and racist violent extremism", precisely so that resources would follow threat rather than assumption.

Second, it created a reciprocal dynamic that has driven a great deal of what followed. The Islamic State's official response to Christchurch explicitly called for revenge attacks. Jihadist and far-right violence in Australia have since fed each other in a reasonably direct way, each atrocity supplying the other's recruiters with their best argument.

The arithmetic of disruption

The attacks in this module are the ones you have heard of. They are a small fraction of the activity.

ASIO has said publicly that between the raising of the threat level in September 2014 and the early 2020s, Australia experienced eleven completed terrorist attacks and disrupted twenty-one significant plots. By mid-2026 the disruption figure being cited had risen to more than thirty. The Commonwealth Director of Public Prosecutions has prosecuted well over a hundred terrorism defendants since 2014 — more in that decade than in the entire preceding history of the offences.

Most of those cases never became famous, for three reasons. They involved young men who had not got very far. They frequently resulted in guilty pleas, so there was no trial to report. And a good number of them were charged with offences — possessing extremist material, funding, attempted travel — that produce short sentences and no dramatic narrative.

There is also a category the public almost never hears about: plots where charges were laid and then quietly did not proceed, or where juries acquitted. In November 2018, three men were arrested in Melbourne over an alleged plan for a mass shooting in a crowded public place; the prosecutions that followed did not all end in conviction. The Anzac Day case produced one conviction and one withdrawal. Holsworthy produced three convictions and two acquittals. Pendennis produced convictions and acquittals in both cities.

Why this matters

Public debate about counter-terrorism tends to assume two states of the world: the plot was stopped, or it wasn't. The reality is a large middle zone in which agencies act on incomplete information, the charge is arguable, and reasonable people — including juries — disagree about whether the person in the dock was dangerous.

That zone is where most of the system's actual work happens, and it is where both the successes and the injustices are found.

Where the decade ended

In November 2022, for the first time since September 2014, ASIO lowered the national terrorism threat level — from Probable back to Possible. The Director-General, Mike Burgess, explained that fewer people were being radicalised, and those who were, were less likely to attack.

The judgement was defensible on the evidence. The Islamic State had lost its territory, its media apparatus and its capacity to direct anything. The travel routes were closed. The 2014–15 cohort was in prison. Australia had gone four years without a fatal jihadist attack.

It lasted twenty-one months.

Module Eight

The Milieu, Part Two

After the caliphate: what replaced the bookshop, and why the average suspect is now a child.

2019–2026 13 min Read the caution
Same caution as Module 3

This module again names people who have not been convicted of any criminal offence. One has been found by a court to have breached a civil anti-discrimination law, which is a different and much lower-threshold finding than a criminal conviction, and that distinction is preserved carefully below.

When the Islamic State lost Baghouz in March 2019, a great many people assumed the milieu would collapse with it. Movements built on the promise of an imminent utopia usually do not survive its very public destruction.

What happened instead is that the milieu changed shape. It lost its institutions and gained a platform. It lost its foreign destination and turned inward. And its median participant got about fifteen years younger.

The last of the physical centres

Why "just ban it" is harder than it sounds

Australia can proscribe an organisation that directly or indirectly engages in, prepares, plans, assists or fosters a terrorist act. It cannot proscribe a shopfront, a preacher or an opinion. Successive attorneys-general have looked at these institutions and concluded the legal test was not met. In December 2025 the government created a new power for the Home Affairs Minister to cancel or refuse visas of "hate preachers" — which addresses the visiting speaker but not the Australian citizen with a lease on a shop in Bankstown.

Australia has also, since 2018, listed a small number of individuals and organisations for other reasons, and in 2026 introduced legislation aimed at enabling the proscription of groups such as Hizb ut-Tahrir and the neo-Nazi National Socialist Network. Whether those provisions survive constitutional challenge is not yet known.

The platform replaces the place

The far more important change is that the physical milieu stopped mattering very much.

Between roughly 2016 and 2020, the centre of gravity moved decisively to encrypted messaging and social platforms — first Telegram, then a much wider array: TikTok, Discord, gaming voice chat, Instagram, and a long tail of small forums. This changed the sociology of radicalisation in four ways that Australian agencies have described repeatedly.

Speed. The old pathway ran over years: attendance, friendship, study circles, gradual escalation. The new pathway can run in weeks. ASIO's public language has shifted accordingly, from "radicalisation" to warnings about people who "move to violence with little warning".

Age. There is no doorman on the internet. A fourteen-year-old cannot easily walk into a prayer room in Bankstown and be taken seriously. A fourteen-year-old on Telegram is indistinguishable from anyone else, and in some of these spaces his enthusiasm makes him a prized recruit.

Incoherence. Online extremist spaces are increasingly promiscuous. Australian security officials now routinely describe "mixed, unstable or unclear" ideologies: young people who consume Islamic State execution videos alongside neo-Nazi accelerationist material alongside school-shooting fandom alongside nihilist gore forums, and who cannot articulate a political programme at all. The category was created because the old ones stopped fitting.

Isolation. A conspiracy has to be organised, and organisation can be intercepted. A boy in a bedroom who decides on Tuesday and acts on Saturday presents almost no attack surface for intelligence. Every attack in Australia since 2020 has been the work of one or two individuals.

The single biggest change in Australian terrorism since 2019 is not ideological. It is that the threat moved from a network that could be mapped to a mood that cannot be.

A hard policy question with no good answer

What do you do with a fourteen-year-old who has downloaded a beheading video and told a Discord server he is going to stab someone?

Charge him, and you put a child into the criminal justice system on a terrorism offence, in a youth detention environment that has an extremely poor record of producing anything other than more committed offenders, and you hand the milieu a martyr. Don't charge him, and if he acts, the failure is unforgivable and public.

Australia has diversion and intervention programs — New South Wales runs schemes for both prisoners and community referrals, and there is federal funding for countering violent extremism. Independent evaluation of their effectiveness is thin, and practitioners themselves generally describe the evidence base as weak. This is, on the current numbers, the most important unsolved problem in the field.

Prison, again

Australia now has a standing population of convicted terrorism offenders, and a legal architecture built around the fact that some of them will finish their sentences.

Since 2016 the Commonwealth has been able to seek a continuing detention order — keeping a convicted terrorist in prison beyond the expiry of the sentence if a court is satisfied of an unacceptable risk — and, since 2021, the lesser extended supervision order. Abdul Nacer Benbrika was the first person subjected to a continuing detention order in Australia, in late 2020; in December 2023 the Victorian Supreme Court released him instead onto a twelve-month extended supervision order. His attempted deportation, and the earlier cancellation of his citizenship — which the High Court found invalid in 2023 — have kept him in the news for two decades.

Preventive detention of people who have completed their sentences is a genuine departure from ordinary criminal law principle. The argument for it is straightforward and hard to dismiss: some of these people say, on the record, that they intend to do it again. The argument against is equally straightforward: it punishes what a person might do, on an assessment of risk that no expert can make reliably, and the same logic would justify indefinite detention of a great many other dangerous offenders.

The platform question

If the milieu now lives on platforms, the obvious response is to make the platforms remove the material. Australia has tried harder than almost any comparable country, and the results are instructive.

After Christchurch — where the attacker livestreamed on Facebook and the footage was reuploaded faster than it could be taken down — Australia passed the Sharing of Abhorrent Violent Material Act within weeks, creating criminal liability for platforms that fail to remove such content expeditiously. It was drafted in five days, criticised by essentially every technology lawyer in the country, and has barely been used. Australia also co-founded the Christchurch Call, an international commitment on terrorist content online.

The most revealing episode came after Wakeley. Video of the bishop being stabbed — livestreamed, and therefore instantly everywhere — was the subject of a removal notice from Australia's eSafety Commissioner in April 2024. Most platforms complied. X, formerly Twitter, geoblocked the material for Australian users but refused to remove it globally, on the argument that one country's regulator cannot dictate what the rest of the world sees. The Federal Court granted an interim injunction; the litigation became a global free-speech cause; and in June 2024 the Commissioner discontinued the case.

The episode settled nothing and demonstrated everything. A liberal democracy with strong online safety laws, acting fast, with judicial support and public backing, could not get an atrocity video off the internet. The video is still findable. So is the Christchurch footage. So is essentially every piece of Islamic State media produced between 2014 and 2019.

Content moderation is a real tool with real effects at the margin. It is not a solution, and treating it as one has absorbed a great deal of the policy energy that might have gone somewhere more useful.

The returnees

Around forty Australians came back from Syria and Iraq. What happened to them is a reasonable test of the system.

Some were prosecuted, where evidence gathered in a war zone could be assembled to an Australian criminal standard — which is extremely difficult, and is why the number of successful foreign-fighter prosecutions is smaller than the number of returnees. Some were placed on control orders. Some were simply monitored. A woman who returned from Syria was charged as recently as 2026.

The striking fact is a negative one: no returnee from Syria or Iraq has carried out a terrorist attack in Australia. The same is broadly true across Western Europe, where the feared "blowback" wave from returning foreign fighters largely did not materialise — the great majority of post-2016 attacks in the West were carried out by people who had never left.

This does not mean the risk was imagined. It does mean the risk was mispriced: enormous political and legal effort went into the returnee problem, while the actual threat was already coming from teenagers who had never held a passport.

What the milieu looks like in 2026

Smaller, in terms of organised infrastructure, than at any point since 2001. There is no Al-Furqan. There is no Global Islamic Youth Centre. The travel routes are closed and there is nowhere to travel to.

But: a handful of shopfront institutions with large online audiences; a legal Islamist party with an antisemitism problem and no criminal exposure; a diffuse online ecology that no Australian agency can meaningfully police; a cohort of convicted offenders now cycling out of prison; a generation of teenagers whose formative political experience has been two years of war in Gaza streamed onto their phones; and a set of grievances that are, in many cases, entirely real and are being harvested by people who have no interest in resolving them.

In August 2024, after twenty-one months at the lower setting, ASIO raised the national terrorism threat level back to Probable. The reasons given were the volatility of social cohesion, the intensity of feeling around the Gaza war, and the number of very young people appearing in the caseload.

Sixteen months later, at Bondi Beach, the assessment was vindicated in the worst way available.

Module Nine

Fracture

A church in Wakeley, two synagogue fires, an Iranian operation, and fifteen dead at Bondi Beach.

Oct 2023 – Aug 2026 17 min Distressing content

On 7 October 2023, Hamas attacked southern Israel, killing about 1,200 people. In the war that followed, tens of thousands of Palestinians were killed in Gaza. Neither of those events happened in Australia. Both of them reordered it.

What follows is the most difficult part of this guide to write fairly, because it is recent, contested, and bound up with a political argument that is still going on. The two years before

From October 2023, recorded antisemitic incidents in Australia rose to levels without precedent in the country's history — graffiti, arson, harassment, threats, attacks on Jewish schools, businesses and homes. Underneath the argument, two things were happening that mattered more. Australian security agencies were recording a marked increase in the number of people, and especially very young people, moving quickly towards violence. And a foreign state was running an operation.

Module Ten

The Ideas, in Brief

A short primer on what these people actually believe — and on the four or five distinctions that make the difference.

Primer 9 min Deliberately brief

This module is short on purpose. The intellectual history of jihadism deserves its own treatment, and a proper one would go back eight centuries. What follows is the working minimum: enough to read the rest of this guide without being misled, and enough to notice when someone on television is talking nonsense.

Four words that are not synonyms

Islam
A religion of roughly two billion people, with fourteen centuries of internal argument, multiple legal schools, and no central authority. Nothing in this guide is a statement about it.
Islamism
A family of modern political movements holding that Islam should shape the state and its law. Most Islamists are non-violent and many participate in elections. Turkey's governing party is Islamist. So, in a very different register, is Hizb ut-Tahrir, which refuses to participate in elections but has never been shown to plan violence.
Salafism
A puritan religious reform movement, emerging in its modern form in the eighteenth century and exported globally with Saudi money from the 1970s. It insists on returning to the practice of the first three generations of Muslims (al-salaf al-salih) and stripping away later accretion. The great majority of Salafis are politically quietist — they regard rebellion against a Muslim ruler as forbidden — and are theologically hostile to the jihadists.
Salafi-jihadism
The tiny militant offshoot that this guide is about. It takes Salafi theology and fuses it with a revolutionary political programme and a doctrine that armed struggle is an individual obligation on every Muslim, right now.

Collapsing these four into one another is the most common analytical error in public discussion of the subject, and it is made by people who are hostile to Islam and by people who are defending it, usually in opposite directions.

The load-bearing concepts

Five ideas do nearly all the work. They are all drawn from real Islamic vocabulary; the jihadist move in each case is to take a term with a long, contested, usually restrictive scholarly history and give it a maximal reading.

1. Tawhid and hakimiyya — sovereignty

Tawhid is the oneness of God, the central doctrine of Islam. The jihadist extension, developed in the twentieth century, is hakimiyya: that God's sovereignty extends to legislation, so that any state making law by human authority — a parliament, a constitution — is claiming a divine prerogative and is therefore not merely bad but idolatrous. This is the argument that turns "I disagree with this government" into "this government is illegitimate at the level of creation".

2. Takfir — excommunication

The declaration that a self-identifying Muslim is in fact an unbeliever. Mainstream Islamic scholarship treats this as extraordinarily dangerous and hedges it with strict conditions, precisely because it licenses killing. Jihadist movements have progressively loosened those conditions — first to rulers, then to soldiers and officials, then to anyone who votes, then in the Islamic State's practice to entire populations. Takfir is why the great majority of the victims of jihadist violence worldwide are Muslims, and why a Melbourne cell firebombed a Shia mosque.

3. Al-wala' wa'l-bara' — loyalty and disavowal

Loyalty to believers, disavowal of unbelievers. In its jihadist reading this becomes a demand for total social separation: do not take non-Muslims as friends, do not celebrate their holidays, do not seek their citizenship, do not accept their laws. It is the doctrinal engine of isolation, and it is what makes the milieu socially self-sealing.

4. Jihad as fard 'ayn — individual obligation

Classical Islamic law overwhelmingly treats armed jihad as fard kifaya — a collective duty, discharged on behalf of the community by a legitimate authority, subject to rules about who may be killed. The pivotal modern move, made most influentially by Abdullah Azzam during the Afghan war of the 1980s, was to declare that when Muslim land is occupied, jihad becomes fard 'ayn: an individual obligation binding on every Muslim personally, requiring no permission from any authority, parent or spouse.

Everything downstream depends on this. It is what converts a boy in Melbourne from a spectator into someone with a personal duty.

5. The caliphate

A single political authority over the global Muslim community, abolished in 1924. Its restoration is the shared goal of essentially all Islamist movements, violent and not. The Islamic State's decisive innovation in 2014 was simply to declare that it had happened — which is why the declaration mattered so much, and why the loss of territory in 2019 was so damaging to a movement that had staked its authority on holding it.

A very compressed genealogy

Two turns on that line matter for Australia specifically.

The first is bin Laden's 1998 decision to prioritise the "far enemy" — the United States and its allies — over the "near enemy" of Arab governments, on the theory that the regimes would fall once their Western patron was driven out. This is what made Western countries targets at all. Australia's participation in Afghanistan and Iraq, and later in the anti-Islamic State coalition, put it explicitly and repeatedly on the target list in jihadist propaganda, and virtually every Australian offender in this guide has cited it.

The second is Adnani's September 2014 statement, discussed in Module 6, which abandoned the whole apparatus of organisation. Kill anyone, anywhere, with anything, without asking. That is the operative doctrine in Australia today, and it is why the Islamic State's al-Naba could describe the Bondi attackers as answering a call while carefully not claiming to have organised anything.

The Australian argument: the covenant of security

There is one theological dispute that has been unusually prominent in Australia and Britain and it is worth knowing.

A well-established position in Islamic law holds that a Muslim who enters a non-Muslim country under its protection — on a visa, or as a citizen — enters a covenant of security (aman), and that treachery against a host who has guaranteed your safety is expressly forbidden. On this reading, whatever one thinks of Australian foreign policy, attacking Australians in Australia is a religious violation, not a religious duty.

This is the argument most commonly deployed by conservative Australian imams against the jihadists, and it is why several very hardline Salafi preachers in this country have consistently opposed domestic attacks while saying nothing that a liberal would recognise as moderate. The jihadist counter-argument — that the covenant is void because Australia is at war with Muslims — is precisely the point at issue in a great many of the recorded conversations in Australian terrorism prosecutions.

A striking amount of Australian counter-terrorism has, in practice, rested on an argument between conservative clerics about a point of medieval contract law.

Why it appeals

Ideology explains what people do once they are in. It does not, on its own, explain why they went in. The research literature — and the Australian case files — point repeatedly at a small set of non-theological drivers.

  • Significance. A profoundly ordinary life, transformed into a cosmic role, immediately and at no cost but risk.
  • Certainty. A total, closed and internally consistent explanation of everything, delivered to people at exactly the age when ambiguity is least tolerable.
  • Belonging. Almost every Australian case involves friendship groups, and a great many involve brothers, cousins and in-laws. People do not join ideologies; they join their friends and acquire the ideology afterwards.
  • Grievance with real content. Iraq, Afghanistan, Guantánamo, Syria, Gaza. The recruiter's most effective material has never been theology. It is footage.
  • Permission. For a small number of people — and this is the uncomfortable one — the ideology supplies retrospective justification for a violence they already wanted. Several of the men in this guide had extensive records for violence against women or strangers long before they had any religious opinions at all.

What it has become

Finally, the current mutation, which is the thing security agencies find hardest to talk about.

The teenage cases now dominating Australian counter-terrorism frequently do not hold the ideology in any recognisable form. They consume Islamic State execution videos alongside neo-Nazi accelerationist material alongside school-massacre archives, in the same feed, on the same afternoon. They cannot state a political objective. They are drawn to atrocity as such.

ASIO now formally categorises a portion of its caseload as involving "mixed, unstable or unclear" ideology. That category exists because the old model — belief produces action — has stopped describing what analysts are seeing. In a meaningful number of contemporary cases, the direction of travel appears to run the other way: the desire to kill comes first, and the ideology is selected afterwards, from a menu, largely on the basis of which one has the best videos.

Module Eleven

Patterns and Arguments

Analysis 13 min Final module

Six patterns

1. It runs through families and friendships, not recruitment

The single most robust finding in the Australian record is that people arrive through people they already know. The Pendennis Sydney cell was built from an uncle, a nephew and a set of neighbours. Khaled Sharrouf and Mohamed Elomar were Pendennis-adjacent, married into each other's families in Syria, and Elomar was the nephew of a convicted plotter and the brother of a Hyde Park rioter. The Khayat brothers plotted to blow up a plane carrying their own brother. Sevdet Besim was a friend of Numan Haider. The Bondi Beach attackers were a father and a son.

This matters practically. It means the network is not permeable to the classic informant strategy, because kinship groups are hard to penetrate. It also means that families are frequently the earliest and best-placed source of warning — and, in a number of Australian cases, actually were.

2. They were almost always already known

Numan Haider: passport cancelled, under active attention. Man Haron Monis: on bail for accessory to murder and forty sexual offences, and had written to ASIO. Yacqub Khayre: acquitted in a terrorism trial seven years earlier, on parole. Hassan Khalif Shire Ali: passport cancelled in 2015. Naveed Akram: examined by ASIO in 2019 in connection with an Islamic State cell.

The failure mode in Australia has not been detection. It has been the transition from "known and assessed" to "no longer of interest". The reason is structural rather than negligent: the pool of people who exhibit concerning behaviour is very much larger than the pool of people who will act, the ratio is somewhere in the thousands to one, and no agency on earth can maintain intensive coverage of everyone who once seemed worrying. Every public inquiry into this problem in every comparable country reaches the same wall.

Australia's gun control settings, the thing the country has been proudest of for thirty years, had a category error in them, and it took fifteen deaths to find it.

6. Direction gave way to inspiration, and inspiration gave way to nothing at all

Three eras, cleanly delineated: 2001–2009, foreign organisations with Australian branches and trained operatives; 2014–2017, remote direction, with a man in Raqqa commissioning attacks in Sydney by phone; 2018 onwards, self-starters with no contact, no permission and no plan beyond the day.

Each transition made the threat less capable and harder to see. The Etihad plot could have killed 400 people and was found by an intelligence partner. The Bondi attack killed 15 and had no interceptable signature at all.

Six arguments Australia has not settled

Does the preventive architecture work?

Australia passed 92 counter-terrorism statutes in twenty years and built the most extensive preventive apparatus in the democratic world: preparatory offences, control orders, preventative detention, questioning warrants, declared areas, continuing detention after sentence, citizenship stripping.

For: Every plot in Modules 2, 4 and 7 was disrupted before anyone died, and several of them — Pendennis, Kastelholm, Silves — would have killed large numbers. Australia's per-capita death toll from jihadist terrorism over twenty-five years is far below that of Britain, France or the United States. Something worked.

Against: The three worst days — Martin Place, Bourke Street, Bondi Beach — were not prevented by any of it. The powers have expanded continuously and contracted essentially never. The High Court has repeatedly found that Parliament went too far. Whole categories of offence criminalise possession and speech. And a substantial share of the people caught by the preparatory offences were, on any fair reading, fantasists rather than operators.

What do you do about lawful extremism?

This is the hardest one and it has no good answer. Australia cannot proscribe an organisation that has not been shown to plan or assist violence. It cannot criminalise an opinion. What it has done instead is reach for adjacent tools: civil racial hatred law, as in the Haddad case; visa cancellation for foreign preachers; funding decisions; charity regulation.

Each of these is a proxy, and proxies have costs. Using anti-discrimination law against a preacher produces a finding that is easily represented inside the milieu as proof that the state is at war with Islam, which is exactly the proposition the preacher was advancing. Doing nothing leaves a pipeline running in plain sight. There is no version of this that does not make something worse.

Mental illness or ideology?

Argued at every inquest. The evidence is that this is a false choice: most lone-actor attackers exhibit both, along with grievance, humiliation and, very frequently, a history of violence against women. The practical stakes are real though — whether a case is handled by health services or by counter-terrorism police determines everything about what happens next, and Australia has been visibly uncertain, most obviously with Monis and again with the sixteen-year-old at Wakeley.

What has the response cost the people it was supposed to protect?

Australian Muslims have spent twenty-five years as what British researchers call a "suspect community": subject to heavy policing, repeated demands for collective condemnation, and a public conversation that has frequently failed to distinguish between a bad sermon and a bomb.

There is a security argument here, not just a fairness one. Intelligence about young people moving towards violence comes overwhelmingly from families and communities. Every measure that makes those families less willing to pick up the phone has a cost that is real and completely invisible in the statistics.

The 740 per cent increase in reported Islamophobic incidents after Bondi Beach — an attack carried out by two men whom Muslim leaders refused even to bury — is the clearest illustration available of how the harm distributes.

What about the people still in the camps?

Covered in Module 5, unresolved as of August 2026. Around 34 Australian citizens, mostly children, are outside Australian jurisdiction with no plan.

Are the ideologies converging?

The most important open analytical question. Between November 2025 and February 2026, Australia recorded five terrorist incidents: one jihadist, three from the far right, one unclassifiable. ASIO has for several years described a growing portion of its caseload as ideologically mixed or unstable.

The optimistic reading is that this reflects the collapse of jihadism as a coherent movement. The pessimistic reading is that a generation is being radicalised into violence as such, from which any available ideology can be retrofitted, and that this is much harder to counter than a doctrine, because you cannot argue with it.

How Australia compares

It is easy to lose perspective inside a single country's story. Set beside comparable democracies over the same twenty-five years, the Australian record has three distinctive features.

Far fewer deaths, until 2025. Britain, France, Spain, Belgium, Germany and the United States all suffered mass-casualty jihadist attacks that Australia did not. Analysts attribute this to a combination of geography (no land borders, difficult to reach), gun laws (which held until Bondi), a smaller and later-arriving Muslim population, aggressive early disruption, and luck. The Etihad plot is the clearest reminder of how much of it was luck.

Far more legislation. Australia passed 92 counter-terrorism statutes in two decades — comfortably more than the United Kingdom, which has faced a much larger threat. Australia is also the only major democracy without a national bill of rights, which means there is no domestic human rights instrument against which such laws can be tested. The check has instead come from the High Court's implied constitutional limits, which are narrow and slow.

Comparable foreign fighter outflow. Proportionate to its Muslim population, Australia sent a similar or slightly higher share to Syria and Iraq than Britain did, and a lower share than Belgium or the Balkans. This is the one metric on which Australia's outcome was not unusually good, and it is the one most closely connected to the milieu described in Modules 3 and 8.

What the record suggests, if anything

This guide is a description, not a policy paper. But if twenty-five years of Australian cases point at anything, it is probably these five things, all of which are boring.

  • The handover problem is the problem. Almost every attacker was known. The recurring failure is at the seam — between agencies, between intelligence and police, between corrections and community, between health and counter-terrorism. That is a management problem, not a powers problem, and no new offence fixes it.
  • Families are the best sensor available, and the response has systematically degraded them. Anything that raises the cost of picking up the phone — heavy-handed policing, collective blame, the certainty that a call means a raid rather than a conversation — is a net security loss.
  • The youth caseload needs something that is not the criminal law, and Australia does not yet have it. Nobody does, but the absence is now the binding constraint.
  • Emergency legislation is usually wrong. The Christmas Eve protest laws lasted four months. The Dural caravan drove weeks of policy on facts that were fabricated. The one durable exception — post-Port Arthur gun reform — took months, not days, and even it had a gap in it that took thirty years to find.
  • The lawful milieu is a real phenomenon and there is no legal instrument that addresses it. This is the honest position. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling either a ban that will not survive the courts or a complacency that the record does not support.

A closing thought

It is worth ending where the numbers actually sit. In twenty-five years, jihadist terrorism has killed twenty people in Australia — fifteen of them on one evening in December 2025. In the same period, more than four times that number of Australians were killed by terrorism abroad, most of them in Bali. In the same period, an Australian citizen murdered fifty-one Muslims at prayer in Christchurch.

These proportions are worth holding onto, because the political weight attached to this subject has never been proportionate to its body count, and that disproportion is itself part of the story. The purpose of terrorism is to be disproportionate. It succeeds by producing a response larger than the act.

What the Australian record actually shows is a small, persistent, mutating problem, handled by an apparatus that is technically capable and institutionally over-extended, in a country whose social cohesion has held up better than most and worse than it likes to believe. It shows a state that legislates in the first fortnight and litigates for a decade afterwards. And it shows, repeatedly — at the Lindt Café, at Wakeley, on the footbridge at Bondi — ordinary people running towards armed men, which is the only genuinely encouraging thing in the entire record.

Glossary

ASIO
Australian Security Intelligence Organisation — the domestic intelligence agency. Collects and assesses; does not arrest.
AFP
Australian Federal Police. Leads terrorism investigations and lays Commonwealth charges.
JCTT
Joint Counter Terrorism Team — standing partnerships of AFP, ASIO and each state police force. The vehicle for almost every operation named in this guide.
Control order
A court order restricting a person not convicted of anything — curfews, electronic monitoring, association and communication bans. Introduced 2005.
Continuing detention order (CDO)
Keeps a convicted terrorist in prison after the sentence expires, on a finding of unacceptable risk. Introduced 2016.
Extended supervision order (ESO)
The community-based alternative to a CDO. Introduced 2021.
Preparatory offence
The workhorse of Australian terrorism prosecution: doing an act in preparation for, or planning, a terrorist act. No specific target need be proved.
Proscription
Formal listing of an organisation as terrorist, making membership and support criminal. Requires a finding that the group engages in, prepares, plans, assists or fosters terrorism.
Threat levels
Not Expected · Possible · Probable · Expected · Certain. Australia has sat at Probable since August 2024, and previously from September 2014 to November 2022 (then styled "High").
Dawah
Invitation, proselytising. Entirely ordinary religious activity; "dawah stalls" and "dawah vans" appear in this guide because a few such operations became milieu hubs.
Takfir
Declaring a Muslim to be an unbeliever. Mainstream scholarship restricts it severely; jihadism has progressively loosened it.
Fard 'ayn / fard kifaya
Individual obligation versus collective obligation. The reclassification of armed jihad from the second to the first is the doctrinal hinge of the whole movement.
Covenant of security (aman)
The classical doctrine that a Muslim who enters a country under its protection may not betray it. The main theological argument used against domestic attacks by conservative Australian clerics.
al-Hawl / al-Roj
Camps in north-eastern Syria holding families associated with Islamic State fighters, including several dozen Australians.

Where to read more

Primary documents
Sentencing remarks and judgments are published on AustLII and on state Supreme Court sites, and are far better than any journalism: R v Lodhi, R v Benbrika, R v Elomar & Ors, R v Khayat, Wertheim v Haddad [2025] FCA 720. The NSW Coroner's Inquest into the deaths arising from the Lindt Café siege (2017) is the single most detailed public document in this whole field.
Official assessments
ASIO's Annual Threat Assessment, published each year since 2020 and freely available, is the best short statement of the current picture. The Independent National Security Legislation Monitor's reports are the best critical account of the legislation.
Academic and analytical
Andrew Zammit has written the most consistently careful open-source analysis of Australian jihadism for well over a decade. The Combating Terrorism Center at West Point has published detailed case studies of the Holsworthy plot and Operation Silves. Greg Barton, Clarke Jones, Levi West, Anne Aly and Lydia Khalil have all written accessibly for general audiences. Hardy and Williams, "Two Decades of Australian Counterterrorism Laws" (2022) is the standard legal survey.
Journalism
ABC Four Corners and Background Briefing have done the most sustained investigative work, including the two-part 2026 Bondi series. The Sydney Morning Herald and The Age court reporting is the most reliable day-to-day record.
Standing caveats

Matters currently before the courts — principally the Bondi Beach prosecution and the Wakeley proceedings — are described here on the basis of charges laid and material released publicly by police and courts. No finding of guilt should be inferred.

Where individuals have not been charged, that is stated explicitly and is not a matter of inference. Where accounts are disputed — the nature of Monis's motivation, the Opera House chant, the ideological content of the Brighton siege — the dispute is described rather than resolved.
